## 3. Why deleting dots raised the score [MODEL + INFERENCE]

With binary dots the official metric collapses to `DTI = T / (0.2·N + 0.8·G)` where `T` is
credit captured and `G ≈ 12,226` the hidden truth mass (H51 inversion):

- At N = 44,090, s = 0.2600: `T = 0.2600·(0.2·44,090 + 0.8·12,226) = 4,836` credit px.
- At N = 37,654, s = 0.2778: `T = 0.2778·(0.2·37,654 + 0.8·12,226) = 4,809` credit px.

The prune **lost ~27 credit pixels (−0.6 %) but deleted 6,436 dots**, shrinking the
denominator by `0.2·6,436 = 1,287`. The removed dots earned ≈0.004 credit each — far below
the marginal bar `0.2·s ≈ 0.052**. [MODEL]**

**Answer [INFERENCE]:** the 0.2778 win was **dot economy** — pruning near-catalogue dots
that sat inside the 300 m support of scored truth without being its best cover — not
geology. Corollaries:

1. The organizer does **not** mask *prediction* pixels near known faults (if near-catalogue
   dots were free, deleting them could not raise the score). Only the *truth* side is
   restricted to new faults. Any dot that is not some truth pixel's best cover pays the full
   0.2 FP rate — including dots on mapped faults.
2. The whole corpus top end (0.24–0.28) spans one structure at different masses; ordering
   follows `N` (Spearman(score, log10 N) = −0.52, p = 0.008). Re-blending that structure
   cannot reach 0.32+.
3. Beating 0.2778 needs `T/N > 0.1097` (corpus-best credit per dot) or coverage beyond the
   corpus's 59 % blanket — i.e. a genuinely different detector, which is what the H52 slate
   (`docs/research/h52-hypotheses.md`) builds.
