Executive summary

What the scores actually say: with binary dots the official distance-weighted Tversky index is exactly DTI = T / (0.2·N + 0.8·G), with N the predicted pixel count, T the credit captured inside the 300 m kernel, and G the hidden truth mass. Inverting a pinned blind-lattice artifact fixes G ≈ 12,226 px (0.24 % of the unmasked footprint).

The local archive contains 25 hash-verified artifact/score records, whose score provenance is not independently authenticated. Within that corpus the best converts 0.1097 credit per dot (with a separate owner-quoted 0.2600 claim), and best estimated coverage is 59.3 %, reached only with 183,642 dots. Conditional on the corpus-derived G estimate, a hypothetical DTI of 0.3195 at 30,000 dots corresponds to about 41.2 % estimated coverage; this does not verify the owner-provided historical 0.3195 or its provenance. Re-blending, re-spacing or pruning the existing corpus cannot establish improved geography; any new detector still needs independent validation.

What this is—and is not

This is a research proxy against existing mapped faults, not the hidden expert-labeled test set. A local pass is necessary for review but is not an organizer score or a promise of prize performance.

The Nevada catalog is CC BY 4.0, but it lacks event-specific location covariance. A precise-looking coordinate is not proof of a precise location; a hypocenter projection is not automatically a surface trace.
